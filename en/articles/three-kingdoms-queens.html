Silla's Three Queens — Seondeok, Jindeok, Jinseong

Silla's three queens — Seondeok, Jindeok, and Jinseong — represent a rare history of female monarchs in ancient East Asia.

The Background of Silla's Female Monarchs

Among the Three Kingdoms, only Silla produced female rulers. In Silla's succession system, the bone-rank system (golpumje) played a decisive role: when no male of the highest rank, the seonggol (Sacred Bone), remained, a seonggol woman could ascend to the throne. This was in essence a straightforward application of bloodline primacy, but in practice it also meant that Silla's aristocratic society had the cultural soil to accept female leadership to some degree. During the Silla period, women's property rights and economic autonomy were recognized relatively broadly, and some aristocratic women maintained independent power bases of their own.

Queen Seondeok — Silla's First Female Ruler

Queen Seondeok (r. 632–647) was the first queen in Korean history. Born a daughter of King Jinpyeong, she was elevated to the throne by aristocratic consensus when no male seonggol heir remained. Her reign was turbulent both at home and abroad. Successive invasions by Baekje and Goguryeo cost Silla territory, yet she strengthened diplomatic ties with Tang China and laid the groundwork for the eventual unification of the Three Kingdoms. Culturally, she presided over a golden age of Buddhist civilization, overseeing the construction of the nine-story wooden pagoda at Hwangnyongsa, the founding of Bunhwangsa temple, and the building of Cheomseongdae — the oldest surviving astronomical observatory in East Asia. She died during the suppression of Bidam's Rebellion, but her diplomatic and cultural policies became the platform from which Unified Silla would rise.

Queen Jindeok and the Transition That Followed

Queen Jindeok (r. 647–654) was Silla's second — and last — seonggol queen. She further solidified the alliance with Tang China and actively adopted Tang court dress and institutions to reorganize Silla's state structure. The "Ode to Great Peace" (Taepyeongseong), which Jindeok reportedly composed herself and sent to Emperor Taizong of Tang, illustrates the closeness of diplomatic relations at the time. After Queen Jindeok's death, Kim Chun-chu (posthumously King Taejong Muyeol) ascended the throne, ending the seonggol era and inaugurating the jingol royal line. Although the two queens reigned for only about 22 years in total, their era overlaps with the decisive turning point at which Silla achieved the unification of the Three Kingdoms, lending it considerable historical weight.

Historical Significance

Silla's female rulers are highly exceptional figures in the ancient history of East Asia. While female rulers did appear in China and Japan as well, what sets Silla's queens apart is that they were legitimate sovereigns by bloodline principle. Joseon-era Confucian historiography sometimes viewed the existence of female rulers as abnormal, but modern scholarship reappraises them as evidence of the relative openness of Silla society and the elevated social standing of women at the time.
