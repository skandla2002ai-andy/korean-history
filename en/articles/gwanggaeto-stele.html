The Gwanggaeto Stele — 1,800 Characters of Controversy

A stone stele erected in Goguryeo in 414 CE. 6.4 meters tall, 1,775 characters. Korea, China, and Japan each read it differently. History carved in stone becomes a battleground of interpretation.

Era Three Kingdoms Period Topic Goguryeo · Historical Debate

Overview

The Gwanggaeto Stele (廣開土大王陵碑) is a monument erected in 414 CE by Goguryeo's King Jangsu to honor his father, King Gwanggaeto the Great (r. 391–413). Located today in Ji'an, Jilin Province, China, it is a massive tuff stele standing approximately 6.4 meters tall and weighing about 37 tons. Its 1,775 Chinese characters record the life and achievements of King Gwanggaeto, his campaigns of conquest, and regulations for tomb guardians (sumoin).

King Gwanggaeto's Conquests

King Gwanggaeto the Great (廣開土大王, 374–413) conquered more territory than any other king in Goguryeo's history. The stele records that over his 22-year reign he captured 64 fortresses and 1,400 villages. He conquered the Khitan and Sushen to the north, Baekje and Wa (Japan) to the south, and Eastern Buyeo to the east. His era name was "Yeongnak" (永樂); adopting an independent era name reflects a consciousness of full sovereign status.

The Inscription Controversy

International academic debate began in the late 19th century when the Japanese Army General Staff acquired rubbings of the stele. The central dispute concerns the interpretation of the "Sinmyo year" (391 CE) passage. Japan read it as "Wa (Japan) crossed the sea and made Baekje and Silla its subjects," using it as evidence for the theory of Japanese colonial rule over the Korean peninsula (Mimana Nihonfu). Korean scholars counter that this reading is based on alteration or misreading of the inscription. China, through its Northeast Project, interprets Goguryeo as a local regime of Chinese history.

Key Facts

Erected: 414 CE · Location: Ji'an, Jilin Province, China · Height: 6.4 m / 1,775 characters · King Gwanggaeto's reign: 391–413 CE · Recorded conquests: 64 fortresses, 1,400 villages
