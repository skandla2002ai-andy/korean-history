Bak Jiwon's Jehol Diary — Pragmatism Learned from Qing China

The travel account Jehol Diary written by Bak Jiwon after his 1780 journey to Qing China. The essence of the Bukhak school of practical learning.

Era Joseon Topic Culture Topic Thought Topic People Related China

Overview

Bak Jiwon (燕巖 朴趾源, 1737–1805) was a writer and thinker who embodied the Bukhak (Northern Learning) school of Silhak (practical learning) in late Joseon. In 1780, he accompanied a diplomatic mission led by his relative Bak Myeong-won, who was traveling to Beijing and Jehol (Chengde) to congratulate the Qing Emperor Qianlong on his seventieth birthday. The record of that journey, the Yolha Ilgi (熱河日記, Jehol Diary), transcends mere travel writing; it is regarded as the greatest prose work of the 18th century — a sharp-eyed observation of Qing China's advanced civilization and technology, and a devastating critique of Joseon's backward reality.

The mainstream intellectuals of Joseon at the time were captivated by "Joseon Sinocentrism," which dismissed Qing as barbarian. But Bak Jiwon and his Bukhak colleagues faced squarely the fact that Qing China actually possessed a far more advanced productive capacity and technological civilization than Joseon, and argued that what could be learned must be learned. This was the meaning of "Bukhak" (北學) — learning from the north, i.e., from Qing China.

Contents and Literary Value of the Jehol Diary

The Jehol Diary is a vast work of 26 volumes. Particularly famous sections include "Hogokjang-non" (好哭場論), capturing the emotions of seeing the Liaodong plains for the first time; "Taehakyugwan-rok," recording conversations through written notes with Qing intellectuals; and detailed descriptions of Qing's advanced technologies such as carts and brick construction. Bak Jiwon presented the use of wheeled transport, brick and tile architecture, and commercial development in Qing as examples Joseon should properly learn from.

Bak Jiwon's prose style attracted attention for departing from the typical Joseon imitation of classical Chinese writing in favor of a vivid and original style. For this very reason, however, King Jeongjo criticized his literary style as eccentric and made him a target of the "literary style rectification" campaign (文體反正). His short stories "Story of Heo Saeng" and "Story of a Yangban" satirized the contradictions of Joseon society in the same vein.

Historical Significance

The significance of the Jehol Diary in the history of Joseon thought lies in its transformation of how reality was perceived. Bak Jiwon confronted directly the fact that maintaining loyalty to the memory of Ming by shunning Qing ultimately deepened the poverty and suffering of the Korean people. The book embodies the essence of Silhak thought — "iyong husaeng" (利用厚生), the idea that technology should be well utilized to enrich the people's lives.

The Jehol Diary also greatly influenced the reform-minded thinkers of the 19th century, and today it is among the most widely read works in the Korean classical prose canon. Bak Jiwon's open scholarly attitude and critical grasp of reality continue to be valued as insights that transcend his time.
