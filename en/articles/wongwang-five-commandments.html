Monk Wongwang's Five Secular Commandments — Ethical Code of the Hwarang

The five precepts Monk Wongwang gave to the Hwarang. A fusion of Buddhist ethics and Confucian values.

Era Three Kingdoms Topic Religion Topic Thought

Monk Wongwang Meets the Hwarang

Monk Wongwang (圓光法師, c. 542–640) was one of the first eminent Buddhist monks produced by Silla. He studied in the Chinese dynasties of Chen and Sui, mastering the Satyasiddhi school and Nirvana studies before returning home. At the time, Silla operated the Hwarang (花郞徒), an elite youth education institution. Around 600 CE, during the reign of King Jinpyeong, two Hwarang warriors, Gwisan (貴山) and Chuhang (箒項), sought out Wongwang and asked him for "teachings they could take as guiding principles for life." Wongwang responded with five precepts — the Five Secular Commandments (世俗五戒) — a fusion of Buddhist bodhisattva precepts and Confucian ethics.

The Five Secular Commandments are as follows. ① Serve the king with loyalty (事君以忠). ② Serve your parents with filial piety (事親以孝). ③ Keep faith with your friends (交友以信). ④ Never retreat in battle (臨戰無退). ⑤ Take life with discrimination (殺生有擇). The last commandment is particularly original: it reinterprets the Buddhist precept against killing in a realistic way suited to an era in which warfare was a constant fact of life.

Content and Nature of the Five Commandments

The Five Secular Commandments are not purely Buddhist precepts; they blend Confucian virtues of loyalty, filial piety, and faithfulness with Buddhist ethics, adapted to fit Silla's warrior culture. The fourth commandment, "never retreat in battle," taught the Hwarang courage on the battlefield, while the fifth, "take life with discrimination," counseled restraint against indiscriminate killing. By presenting these two commandments together, the Hwarang were required to be fierce warriors while also exercising moral judgment. Wongwang said that "keeping these five is sufficient," yet he was candid enough to acknowledge that, having adjusted the precept against taking life to fit secular circumstances, the commandments were "not the complete bodhisattva precepts."

The Samguk Sagi records that Gwisan and Chuhang later put the Five Secular Commandments into practice in the 602 Battle of Amaksan Fortress, fighting until they fell in combat. The Five Commandments became the official ethical code of the Hwarang, serving as the spiritual foundation for Silla's soldiers throughout the wars of unification.

Historical Significance

The Five Secular Commandments vividly illustrate a defining characteristic of Silla Buddhism: Buddhism going beyond the religion of personal liberation to actively participate in state governance and military ethics. By recasting Buddhist ethics to fit Silla's reality — war, loyalty to the state, and communal solidarity — Wongwang helped Buddhism gain wide acceptance among the ruling class and the military. This tradition forms one of two currents in Silla Buddhist culture, alongside Wonhyo's later movement to spread Buddhism among the common people.
