Yi Jung-seop — Bull Paintings and Tin-Foil Letters

Painter Yi Jung-seop, a Korean War refugee. The bull paintings and letters to his family scratched onto cigarette-pack tin foil.

Era Modern Topic Person Topic Culture

A Tragic Painter of Bulls and Children

Yi Jung-seop (1916–1956) is one of the defining figures of modern Korean art, celebrated above all for his paintings of bulls—rendered in bold, dynamic strokes—and his images of children. Born in Pyeongwon, South Pyeongan Province, he studied Western painting at the Bunka Gakuin in Tokyo before returning to Korea and throwing himself into his work. The Korean War and grinding poverty shaped both his life and his art in equal measure.

War, Separation from Family, and Creation

When the Korean War broke out, Yi Jung-seop fled south with his Japanese wife Yamamoto Masako (Korean name Yi Nam-deok) and their two sons. Around 1951 he spent roughly a year with his family in Seogwipo on Jeju Island—a period he later remembered as the happiest of his life—but financial hardship eventually forced him to send his wife and children to Japan. Even in the extreme poverty that followed, he never stopped creating, using the tin-foil inner lining of cigarette packs as a canvas when he had nothing else. The memories of the Seogwipo days are preserved whole in works such as "Fantasy in Seogwipo."

Lonely Final Years and Death

A solo exhibition held at the Midopa Gallery in Seoul in 1955 drew wide acclaim, but his circumstances did not improve. He never managed to reunite with his family. Suffering from severe malnutrition and mental illness, he died in 1956 at the Red Cross Hospital in Seodaemun, Seoul. He was forty years old. The hundreds of works he left behind have since been reappraised as treasures of Korean art.

Historical Significance

Yi Jung-seop was a painter who lived through the full weight of colonization, war, and national division, and his work holds both the vitality and the grief of the Korean experience. By channeling the nation's toughness and sorrow through the image of the bull, he made a lasting contribution to the formation of a distinct identity for modern Korean art.
