Seokguram Grotto — A Miracle in Stone, Shaped by Silla Science

Construction began in 751. Granite blocks interlocked without mortar and held for over 360 years. Then Japanese colonizers applied cement in the 1910s — and that is when the problems began.

Era Unified Silla Topic Buddhism · Architecture

Overview

Seokguram (石窟庵) is a stone grotto temple from the Unified Silla period, located on Mount Toham in Gyeongju, North Gyeongsang Province. Construction was begun in 751 (the 10th year of King Gyeongdeok) by minister Kim Daeseong (金大城) and completed in 774 (the 10th year of King Hyegong). Granite stones were precisely cut and assembled to create an artificial grotto; the main Buddha inside is a fully rounded statue standing 3.4 meters tall. In 1995 it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site together with Bulguksa Temple.

Scientific Design

The main chamber of Seokguram features a hemispherical dome structure: 360 granite slabs are interlocked without mortar and support themselves through their own weight. The lotus-patterned capstone of the ceiling and the precise proportions of the corridor connecting the main chamber to the antechamber are remarkable. The position of the main Buddha and the angle of the skylight were designed so that on the winter solstice, the rising sun illuminates the forehead of the statue exactly.

The Paradox of Japanese-Era Restoration

When the grotto was discovered and disassembled for restoration by Japanese authorities in the 1910s, cement was used in the reassembly. The cement blocked the natural porosity of the original stone, causing moisture to accumulate inside. Today, artificial air-conditioning and dehumidification systems regulate humidity. Scholars argue that had the original structure been preserved intact, natural ventilation would have prevented the problem entirely.
