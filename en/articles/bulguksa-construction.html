The Founding of Bulguksa — The Pinnacle of Silla Buddhist Architecture

Founded in 751 by Kim Daeseong during the reign of King Gyeongdeok, Bulguksa Temple houses Seokgatap and Dabotap — the finest stone architecture of Silla.

Era Unified Silla Topic Culture Topic Religion Region Gyeongju

Overview

Bulguksa (佛國寺) is the largest Buddhist temple of Silla, situated at the foot of Mount Toham in Gyeongju, North Gyeongsang Province. Major construction was initiated in 751 (the 10th year of King Gyeongdeok) at the vow of minister Kim Daeseong (金大城). The Samguk Yusa records that Kim Daeseong built Bulguksa for the parents of his present life, and Seokguram for the parents of his previous life. After Kim Daeseong died in 774, construction continued under state direction until completion. The Bulguksa that stands today was rebuilt after being destroyed by fire during the Imjin War, but its stone structures — Seokgatap, Dabotap, Cheongunggyo, and Baekungyo — retain their original Silla form.

Background of the Founding

In the 8th century, Unified Silla reached the peak of Buddhist culture on the foundation of political stability and economic prosperity. King Gyeongdeok (r. 742–765) actively adopted Tang Chinese civilization and reorganized the state system; during this period great Buddhist works such as the Hwangnyongsa Bell and the Emile Bell (Sacred Bell of King Seongdeok) were undertaken one after another. The founding of Bulguksa was also the product of a religious yearning by the royal family and aristocracy to manifest the Buddhist ideal world — the "Buddha Land" (佛國土) — on earth. The tiered construction on the slopes of Mount Toham, using the incline to create ascending platforms, symbolically expressed the boundary between the Pure Land and the mundane world through the physical space of the temple.

The Cheongunggyo (Blue Cloud Bridge, 33 steps) and Baekungyo (White Cloud Bridge, 16 steps) at the temple entrance embody the path from the secular world up into the Buddha Land, while the Lotus Bridge and Chilbogyo form another sacred passageway leading to Geuknakjeon Hall. Standing side by side in the courtyard before Daeungjeon Hall, Seokgatap (Three-Story Stone Pagoda, National Treasure No. 21) and Dabotap (National Treasure No. 20) translate into architecture the scene in the Lotus Sutra where Shakyamuni and Prabhutaratna sit together to confirm the teaching. Though utterly different in form and structure, their symmetrical placement represents the pinnacle of Silla pagoda artistry.

Seokgatap and the Dharani Scripture

During repairs to Seokgatap in 1966, the Mugujeonggwang Daedarani Gyeong (無垢淨光大陀羅尼經, Pure Light Dharani Scripture) was discovered inside the second story of the pagoda. Estimated to have been produced between 704 and 751, it is recognized as the world's oldest surviving woodblock-printed document. This scroll-format scripture is a precious artifact attesting both to the spiritual world at the time of Bulguksa's founding and to the level of printing technology achieved by Unified Silla.

Historical Significance

Bulguksa was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1995 together with Seokguram. Beyond a simple religious facility, the temple is the architectural expression of the Buddhist ideal world that Silla people sought to realize — as well as the essence of 8th-century East Asian stone construction technology. The tiered temple layout exploiting the hillside, the stone platform walls built from dressed natural rock, and the two pagodas that employ entirely different architectural languages while achieving harmony are still regarded today as the archetype of Korean architecture.
