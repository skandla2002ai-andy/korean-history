King Jinheung — Silla's Greatest Territorial Expansion

King Jinheung transformed Silla, the last of the Three Kingdoms to develop, into a great power. His boundary stelae and sweeping conquests.

Era Three Kingdoms Topic People Topic War Topic Territory

Overview

King Jinheung (眞興王, 534–576) was the 24th ruler of Silla. During his reign (540–576), he secured the Han River basin, pushed north into Hamgyong Province, and absorbed Gaya — achieving the greatest territorial extent in Silla's history. To mark these conquests, he erected boundary stelae (sunsubi, 巡狩碑) at newly acquired sites including Bukhansan, Hwangchoryeong, Maungnyeong, and Changnyeong; these stones are now primary sources for Three Kingdoms historiography. He was also a patron of culture who formalized the Hwarang youth corps as a state institution and actively promoted Buddhism.

The Process of Territorial Expansion

In 551, Jinheung allied with Baekje's King Seong to seize the upper Han River valley from Goguryeo. But in 553 he broke the alliance and seized even the lower Han — territory Baekje had taken. The enraged King Seong invaded Silla the following year, only to be killed at the Battle of Gwansan Fortress (present-day Okcheon). With that, Silla controlled the entire Han River and gained direct maritime access to China.

In 562, Silla annexed Daegaya, completing the subjugation of the Gaya confederacy along the Nakdong River. To the north, Jinheung advanced as far as Hwangchoryeong and Maungnyeong in Hamgyong Province, erecting stelae there. Silla's territory more than doubled during this period, forming the geographic foundation for the later unification of the Three Kingdoms.

Historical Significance

Jinheung's seizure of the Han River basin decisively shifted the balance of power among the Three Kingdoms. The Han valley was the most fertile agricultural region of the peninsula and a key node for sea trade with China. Drawing on this territory, Silla built sufficient power to ally with Tang China in the 7th century and successively destroy Baekje (660) and Goguryeo (668), achieving unification. Jinheung's boundary stelae are designated National Treasures; the Bukhansan stele (on Bibong Peak) was identified and deciphered by the scholar Kim Jeong-hui in 1816.
