Gyeongju: A Millennium Capital — Legacy of the Silla Kingdom

Gyeongju served as Silla's capital for nearly a thousand years, from 57 BC to 935 AD. A royal city sleeping beneath the earth.

Era Three Kingdoms Era Unified Silla Topic History Topic Culture Region Gyeongju

A Capital for a Thousand Years

Gyeongju served as the capital of Silla from the kingdom's founding by Bak Hyeokgeose in 57 BC until King Gyeongsun's surrender to Goryeo in 935 — a reign of approximately 992 years. In classical Chinese sources it was also known as Geumseong (金城), Seorabeol (徐羅伐), and Gyerim (鷄林). At its height in the 8th and 9th centuries, the Samguk Yusa records the city's population at some 170,000 households — nearly one million people — with tile-roofed houses filling the capital and the smoke of cooking fires never ceasing.

City Layout and Key Heritage Sites

Gyeongju used Wolseong Fortress as its palace and arranged aristocratic residences and Buddhist temples in every direction. Hwangnyongsa Temple (founded 553), with its enormous nine-story wooden pagoda, served as the center of state ritual until it was burned by Mongol forces in 1238. Bulguksa Temple and Seokguram Grotto (751, built during the reign of King Gyeongdeok) represent the pinnacle of Unified Silla Buddhist art and were designated UNESCO World Heritage Sites in 1995. Some 155 royal tumuli are distributed across the city center; Cheonmachong (Tumulus No. 155) yielded a birch-bark horse saddle flap painted with a celestial horse, revealing the heights of Silla's visual arts.

Legacy as a World Heritage City

The Gyeongju Historic Areas were designated a UNESCO World Cultural Heritage site in 2000. Key landmarks include the rock-carved Buddhas and cliff reliefs of Namsan, Poseokjeong Pavilion, Cheomseongdae Observatory (c. 632), and Anapji Pond (Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond). The international character of Silla culture also merits attention: tombs have yielded glass vessels and gold earring styles brought from Central Asia, and the legend of Cheoyong hints at the immigration of Arab traders. Gyeongju was a cosmopolitan city that marked the eastern terminus of ancient East-West trade routes.
