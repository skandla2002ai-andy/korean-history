Buddhism Comes to the Three Kingdoms — Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla

Buddhism arrived on the Korean Peninsula beginning with Goguryeo in 372 CE. Each kingdom received it differently.

Era Three Kingdoms Topic Religion Topic History Related China

Buddhism Reaches Goguryeo

Buddhism first arrived on the Korean Peninsula in the latter half of the 4th century CE. According to the Samguk Sagi, in the second year of King Sosurim of Goguryeo (372 CE), the ruler of Former Qin (前秦), Fu Jian, sent the monk Sundo (順道) with Buddhist statues and scriptures. Two years later in 374, the monk Ado (阿道) arrived, and in 375 King Sosurim built the temples Chomun-sa and Ibullan-sa to house them — the first recorded construction of Buddhist temples on the Korean Peninsula. Since Goguryeo was actively engaged with the northern Chinese region at the time, the reception of Buddhism was part of a broader process of absorbing advanced Chinese civilization as a whole.

Baekje and Silla Receive Buddhism

Buddhism reached Baekje in the first year of King Chimnyu's reign (384 CE), transmitted by the Indian monk Marananta (摩羅難陀) from Eastern Jin. King Chimnyu built a temple in Hansan the following year and ordained ten monks. Baekje Buddhism subsequently played an important role as a conduit for the transmission of Buddhism to Japan, and Baekje's Buddhist culture produced outstanding works such as the Gilt-Bronze Incense Burner and the stone pagoda of Mireuksa Temple site. The official recognition of Buddhism in Silla came latest — in the 14th year of King Beopheung's reign (527 CE). The famous account of the monk Ichadon (異次頓), who voluntarily sought martyrdom despite aristocratic opposition to bring about the king's acceptance of Buddhism, is the most celebrated anecdote in Silla Buddhist history. The miracle narrative — that when Ichadon was beheaded, white milk (or blood) spurted from his neck — symbolizes the Silla people's conversion to Buddhism.

The State Character of Buddhism in the Three Kingdoms

A defining feature of Buddhism in the Three Kingdoms is that it was adopted from the outset under royal and state leadership. Buddhism was utilized as an ideological tool for strengthening royal authority and national integration, with a strongly "state-protecting" (護國) character. Hwangnyongsa Temple in Silla and the large temples of Goguryeo and Baekje were not merely religious facilities but centers of national ritual. The "Five Secular Precepts" (世俗五戒) presented by the Buddhist monk Wongwang (圓光) to the Hwarang warriors combined Buddhist ethics with loyalty to the state, forming the human foundation for Silla's unification of the Three Kingdoms.

The Foundation of Korean Buddhist Culture

Buddhism, adopted during the Three Kingdoms period, became the state religion of the Goryeo dynasty and laid the foundations of a millennium of Korean Buddhist culture. Silla's great monks — Wonhyo (元曉) and Uisang (義湘) among them — produced original works that left their mark on the intellectual history of East Asian Buddhism. In the process by which Buddhism was transmitted from the Three Kingdoms to Japan, the Korean Peninsula played a vital role as a bridge for East Asian Buddhist civilization.
