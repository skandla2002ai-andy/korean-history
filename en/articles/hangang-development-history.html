The Han River Development History — How Seoul's Lifeline Was Transformed

The comprehensive Han River development plan of the 1960s–80s: how sandy beaches and islands disappeared and apartment complexes took their place.

Era Modern Topic History Topic Society Region Hanyang & Seoul

The Han River's Historical Role

The Han River, stretching 514 kilometers across the center of the Korean Peninsula from east to west, has been the central axis of Korean life and history since ancient times. During the Three Kingdoms period, Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla competed fiercely for control of the Han River basin, which served as a vital source of irrigation water and a key transportation artery. Baekje established its capital Wiryeseong along the Han River; Silla, after securing the Han River basin, opened a route to Tang China and laid the groundwork for unifying the Three Kingdoms. During the Joseon dynasty, the ferries at Mapo, Ttukseom, and Gwangnarugae flourished as the river served as the supply lifeline for Hanyang (Seoul), carrying tax grain from the southern provinces to the capital.

The Japanese Colonial Period and Postwar Han River

The first modern development of the Han River began during the Japanese colonial period. The Hangang Railway Bridge opened in 1900, followed by the Hangang Pedestrian Bridge (now Hangang Bridge) in 1917. The Government-General also conducted embankment works for flood control and irrigation. The Korean War brought the destruction of most Han River bridges, which were repaired during the postwar reconstruction period. Until the 1960s, the Han River's banks were a mixture of sandy beaches and vegetable fields — a semi-rural zone where city residents swam on the white sand beaches in summer.

The Comprehensive Han River Development Project

The project that fundamentally reshaped the Han River was the Comprehensive Han River Development Project, carried out between 1982 and 1986. With the 1986 Asian Games and 1988 Seoul Olympics approaching, the Chun Doo-hwan government undertook a massive redevelopment aimed at making the Han River a symbol of Seoul as an international city. Floodplain areas at Jamsil, Yeouido, Banpo, and Ttukseom were landscaped and civic parks were created; the Olympic Expressway and Gangbyeon Expressway were built along both banks. Underwater weirs at Singok and Jamsil were installed to regulate the river's water level. The project deepened the river's channel and improved water quality, but the traditional sandy beaches vanished in the process.

The "Han River Miracle" and Today

The "Miracle on the Han River" is the phrase used to symbolize Korea's rapid economic growth in the 1960s–80s, coined in analogy with West Germany's "Miracle on the Rhine." As Gangnam was developed, apartment complexes rose along the Han River banks, shaping the modern urban landscape of Seoul. Today, Han River Park draws tens of millions of visitors each year as Seoul's largest public space, with attractions such as Saevitseom Island and the Banpo Bridge Rainbow Fountain becoming tourist landmarks. The Han River remains a central axis of Seoul's urban planning — and a living witness to modern Korean history.
