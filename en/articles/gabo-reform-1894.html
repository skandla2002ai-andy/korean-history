Gabo Reform 1894 — Korea's Modernization Laws

The Gabo Reform of 1894: abolition of the class system, abolition of the civil service exam, and the emergence of modern legislation.

Era Late Joseon Topic Politics Topic Society Related Japan

Overview

The Gabo Reform (甲午改革) was a series of modern institutional reforms carried out in three phases from July 1894 to February 1896. The Joseon government established the Deliberative Council (軍國機務處) and promulgated sweeping legislation dismantling feudal institutions — including the class system, the civil service examination, and the fragmented fiscal structure. The reform was driven by the confluence of the Donghak Peasant Movement and the international crisis of the First Sino-Japanese War, and is regarded as a structural turning point toward a modern Joseon.

Background

In the spring of 1894, Donghak Peasant Army forces rose in the Jeolla region and captured Jeonju Fortress; the Joseon government requested Qing intervention. Japan simultaneously dispatched troops under the terms of the Tianjin Convention, leading to a standoff between Chinese and Japanese forces on the peninsula. After a peace agreement with the peasant army ended the immediate crisis, Japan — rather than withdrawing — used the pretext of internal reform to seize Gyeongbokgung Palace and install a pro-Japanese cabinet. The Gabo Reform thus bears a dual character: Korea's own demand for change fused with Japan's strategic agenda.

Main Reform Measures

In approximately two months from July 1894, the Deliberative Council processed 208 resolutions (First Reform). The key measures included: abolition of the hereditary social class system (yangban, jungin, commoner, and slave distinctions); abolition of the traditional civil service examination (gwageo) and introduction of a modern merit-based appointment system; prohibition of child marriage and permission for widows to remarry; abolition of guilt by association; and consolidation of state finances under a single ministry (Takjiamunseo). The Second Reform (late 1894) promulgated the Hongbeom 14 Articles, introduced a cabinet system, separated the modern judicial system, and reorganized local administration (from 8 provinces to 23 districts). The Third Reform (1895) laid the foundations for a modern education system through the Education Rescript, the Elementary School Ordinance, and the establishment of Hanseong Normal School.

Historical Significance

The Gabo Reform legally dismantled the centuries-old social hierarchy and civil service system, bringing about a structural transformation of Joseon society. Its limitations, however, are equally notable: the reforms were carried out under Japanese pressure and largely lacked broad popular support, imposed from above rather than growing from below. The pro-Japanese cabinet eventually collapsed following the Eulmi Incident (1895) and the Agwan Pacheon (1896), and some reforms were reversed. Nevertheless, the principle of equality before the law and the modern institutional framework established by the Gabo Reform served as the foundational starting point for Korea's modern state through the Korean Empire, the Japanese colonial period, and into the present.
