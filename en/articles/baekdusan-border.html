The Baekdusan Border — The 1712 Stele and the Present

In 1712, Joseon and Qing carved a boundary on Baekdusan: "west to the Amnok River, east to the Tumen River." Korea and China still read "Tumen" differently today.

Era Joseon · Modern Topic Territory · Diplomacy

Overview

Baekdusan (白頭山, known in Chinese as Changbaishan), a volcano situated on the border between the Korean Peninsula and China, stands at 2,744 m — the highest peak on the peninsula. In 1712 (the 38th year of King Sukjong's reign), Joseon and the Qing dynasty erected a boundary stele (定界碑, jeonggye-bi) near the summit of Baekdusan to demarcate the border. The inscription reads: "To the west, the Amnok River (Yalu); to the east, the Tumen River (土門)." The location of this "Tumen River" was interpreted differently by Joseon (Korea) and the Qing (China).

The Tumen River Dispute

Joseon interpreted the Tumen River as a tributary of the upper Songhua River, asserting that the Gando (Jiandao) region was therefore Joseon territory. The Qing interpreted the Tumen River as the present-day Tumen (Duman) River. In 1909, Japan's Gando Convention with Qing China settled the matter by equating the Tumen with the Duman River, recognizing Gando as Qing territory — in exchange for Japan receiving rights over the South Manchuria Railway. The Republic of Korea maintains that the Gando Convention is null and void, as it was concluded while Korea's diplomatic sovereignty had been stripped by the Eulsa Treaty.

Current Situation

In the 2000s, North Korea and China concluded a border treaty dividing Lake Cheonji (Tianchi) on Baekdusan in approximately a 45:55 ratio (1962 treaty, 1964 protocol). The Republic of Korea was not a party to this agreement. Baekdusan holds special significance for Koreans as the sacred mountain of the nation.

Key Facts

Height of Baekdusan: 2,744 m · Boundary stele erected: 1712 · Gando Convention: 1909 (Japan–Qing) · North Korea–China border treaty: 1962 · Cheonji division: approx. 45% North Korea, 55% China
