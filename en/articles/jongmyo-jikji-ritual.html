Jongmyo Shrine Rites — A Royal Ceremony Recognized by UNESCO

Jongmyo enshrines the spirit tablets of Joseon's kings and queens. The global significance of the Jongmyo Jerye and Jongmyo Jeryeak.

Era Joseon Topic Culture Topic Religion Region Hanyang · Seoul

Jongmyo — The Royal Dynastic Shrine

Jongmyo (宗廟) is the dynastic shrine housing the spirit tablets (神主, wooden memorial tablets) of Joseon's kings and queens, located in Jongno-gu, Seoul. It was one of the first structures King Taejo Yi Seonggye built after moving the capital to Hanyang in 1394, erected alongside Gyeongbokgung Palace. In accordance with Confucian ritual codes, Jongmyo was positioned to the east (left) of the capital, while the Sajikdan altars (for the spirits of land and grain) stood to the west (right). The main hall, Jeongjeon, currently enshrines the tablets of 19 kings and 30 queens, while the secondary hall Yeongnyeongjeon holds 34 additional tablets. The Jeongjeon is among the longest single wooden structures in the world and was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1995.

The Structure and Procedure of Jongmyo Jerye

Jongmyo Jerye (宗廟祭禮) was the highest state ritual, presided over by the king in person. In the Joseon period it was held five times a year — at the four seasons plus the winter solstice sacrifice — but today it is performed on the first Sunday of May each year. The sequence begins with welcoming the spirits (yeongsin), followed by offering silk (jeonpye), presenting food (jinchan), three rounds of offering wine (choheona, aheona, jongheon), sharing the ritual food (eumbok), burning the invocation and offerings (mangnyo), and finally sending off the spirits (songsin). The ceremony proceeded according to strict ritual protocol, involving hundreds of participants from the king down to the officiating ministers.

Jongmyo Jeryeak — Music and Dance Combined

The Jongmyo Jerye is inseparable from the Jongmyo Jeryeak (宗廟祭禮樂), the ritual music. Perfected during the reigns of Kings Sejong and Sejo, it comprises two suites: Botaepeong (保太平), praising civil virtue, and Jeongdaeop (定大業), praising military achievements. It draws on virtually the full range of Korean traditional instruments — pyeonjong (stone chimes), pyeongyeong (metal chimes), piri (oboe), daegeum (transverse flute), haegeum (fiddle), and janggu (hourglass drum). The music is accompanied by Palilmu (八佾舞), a grand court dance performed by 64 dancers. Both the Jongmyo Jerye and the Jeryeak were inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2001, receiving international recognition.

Historical Significance

Jongmyo Jerye was not merely an ancestral rite but served to establish dynastic legitimacy, consolidate royal authority, and bind together the national community. Today the ceremony is held annually under the auspices of the Jeonju Yi Clan's Daedong Jongak Institute, continuing as a living tradition of Joseon royal culture.
