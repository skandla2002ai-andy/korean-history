What Baekje Gave to Japan — The Reality of Cultural Transmission

4th–7th centuries. Baekje transmitted Buddhism, Chinese writing, pottery, and weaving techniques to Yamato Japan. The origins of Japan's Asuka culture.

Era Three Kingdoms Topic Culture · Diplomacy Related Japan

Overview

Baekje (百濟, 18 BCE – 660 CE) maintained close diplomatic and cultural ties with the Yamato court throughout the Three Kingdoms period, serving as a cultural bridge that transmitted advanced civilization from the Korean Peninsula and the continent to Japan. Even Japanese historians acknowledge that Baekje's influence was decisive in shaping Japan's Asuka culture.

Introduction of Buddhism

In 552 CE (or 538 CE), King Seong of Baekje sent Buddhist statues and scriptures to the Yamato court — the official record of Buddhism's introduction to Japan, as documented in the Nihon Shoki. Thereafter, Baekje sent monks, temple architects, and Buddhist sculptors to Japan. Temples from the Asuka and Nara periods, such as Horyuji in Nara, show strong influence from Baekje architectural styles.

Transmission of Learning and the Arts

Records indicate that the scholar Wangin (王仁) transmitted the Analects of Confucius and the Thousand Character Classic to Japan in the 5th century. Various craftsmen sent from Baekje — potters, weavers, carpenters, and calendar specialists — served the Yamato royal court. The skills they brought formed the foundation of ancient Japanese state-building.

Key Facts

Official introduction of Buddhism: 552 CE (or 538 CE) · Scholar Wangin: transmitted the Analects and Thousand Character Classic in the early 5th century · Upon Baekje's fall in 660, many nobles and craftsmen fled to Japan · Baekje artistic styles are reflected throughout Asuka and Nara culture
