Tamna Kingdom — Jeju Island's Independent Kingdom

Tamna Kingdom maintained independence for 1,400 years, from the Three Kingdoms period until it was absorbed into Goryeo.

Era Three Kingdoms Topic History Topic Politics Region Jeju

Foundation Myth and Early History

The origins of Tamna Kingdom are traced to the Samseonghyeol (Three Clans' Cave) myth of Jeju. According to this legend, three divine men — Go Eul-na, Yang Eul-na, and Bu Eul-na — emerged from the earth, married three princesses from the kingdom of Byeokrangguk, and began raising livestock and farming. The myth symbolizes Tamna as a community with its own distinct culture. In written records, the kingdom first appears under the name Juho (州胡) in the Dongyi section of the Sanguo Zhi (Records of the Three Kingdoms), described as maritime traders who wore leather clothing and herded cattle and pigs.

Relations with the Three Kingdoms and the Preservation of Independence

Records indicate that Tamna paid tribute to Baekje in 476 CE, and later submitted to Silla in the second year of King Munmu (662 CE), receiving the titles of Seonju (Star Lord) and Wangja (Prince). However, submission did not mean annexation. Tamna's rulers retained the independent title of Seonju and maintained diplomatic relations by sending regular envoys to Silla and later to Goryeo. When Goryeo's founder Wang Geon came to power, Tamna again sent tribute while preserving its autonomy. The name Tamna was changed to Jeju (濟州) in the tenth year of Goryeo King Sukjong (1105 CE), at which point the island was reorganized as a commandery (gun), officially ending the history of Tamna as an independent kingdom.

Cultural Legacy and Significance

The distinct culture Tamna left behind lives on throughout Jeju Island today. Spring and autumn rites continue at the Samseonghyeol shrine and the Samseong Shrine, and clan associations of the Go, Yang, and Bu families — who claim descent from the three founding divine men — still exist. Jeju's haenyeo (women divers) culture, the stone grandfather statues called dol hareubang, and the island's unique dialect are all regarded as indigenous traditions shaped since the Tamna era. The Jeju haenyeo culture, inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity list in 2016, stands as global recognition of a way of life that has continued unbroken since the days of Tamna.
