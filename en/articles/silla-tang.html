Silla–Tang Diplomatic Relations

The diplomatic relations between Unified Silla and Tang China, and the impact those relations had on the Korean peninsula.

Region Gyeongju Era Unified Silla Related Tang China Topic Diplomacy Topic Politics

Overview

The relationship between Silla and Tang China is one of East Asian history's most dramatic examples of alliance and rupture. Beginning with Kim Chunchu's (later King Muyeol) visit to Tang in 648, spanning the unification of the Three Kingdoms, and ending with the expulsion of Tang forces from the peninsula — within fewer than thirty years Silla transformed its relationship with Tang from alliance to war.

648 — Kim Chunchu's Diplomatic Mission to Tang

In 648 (the second year of Queen Jindeok), Silla's de facto ruler Kim Chunchu traveled to Chang'an to meet Tang Emperor Taizong in person. The circumstances were desperate. King Uija of Baekje had seized more than forty Silla border fortresses (including the fall of Daeyaseong in 642, where Chunchu's son-in-law and daughter were killed), and a request for military support from Goguryeo had been refused. Tang Taizong, in the process of reassessing his eastern policy after the failed Goguryeo campaign (the 645 Battle of Ansi Fortress), accepted Silla's overture and formed a military alliance, reportedly promising that "after the destruction of Baekje and Goguryeo, all land south of Pyongyang will be given to Silla." Silla agreed to adopt Tang's calendar and court dress. It was a diplomatic success, but Silla surrendered some measure of its autonomy.

660 and 668 — The Fall of the Three Kingdoms

In 660, a Tang army of 130,000 under Su Dingfang combined with 50,000 Silla troops to destroy Baekje; King Uija was taken to Tang. Eight years later in 668, the Silla–Tang alliance destroyed Goguryeo and captured King Bojang. Four centuries of tripartite division came to an end. But Tang did not keep its promises. It established the Ungjin Commandery (Ungdo-dofu) over the former Baekje territory, the Andong Protectorate over Goguryeo, and even attempted to place Silla under direct control through the Gyerim Protectorate — making clear its intent to dominate the entire peninsula.

675–676 — The Silla–Tang War and the Expulsion of Tang

King Munmu of Silla changed strategy: apologizing to Tang to buy time while absorbing Baekje and Goguryeo refugees and building military strength. In 675, at the Battle of Maeso Fortress (thought to be in present-day Yeoncheon, Gyeonggi Province), Silla forces routed a 200,000-strong Tang army, capturing 30,380 warhorses and vast quantities of weapons. In 676, at the naval Battle of Gibeolpo (thought to be near present-day Janghan, South Chungcheong Province), the Tang fleet under Xue Rengui was defeated in twenty-two engagements. Tang withdrew the Andong Protectorate to Liaodong and effectively retreated from the peninsula. The unification of the Three Kingdoms was complete — its southern boundary being the Daedong River.

After Unification — Tribute, Investiture, and Coexistence

Even after the war, relations between Silla and Tang continued. Silla sent envoys and tribute to Tang, and Tang invested Silla's kings. The tribute-investiture relationship was not one of complete subordination but rather a diplomatic protocol of East Asia; Silla accepted the form while maintaining practical autonomy. Silla communities formed in Tang China — Sillabang (Silla residential quarters), Sillaso (Silla administrative offices), and Sillawon (Silla temples) — existed on the Shandong Peninsula and in the Jiangnan region. In the ninth century, Jang Bogo leveraged this network of Silla expatriates to dominate East Asian maritime trade. Choe Chiwon (857–?), who passed the Tang civil examination and made a name as a literary figure before returning home, became the representative Confucian scholar of late Silla.
