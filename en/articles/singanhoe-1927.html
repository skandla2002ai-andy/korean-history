Singanhoe — Left-Right Coalition for Independence

Founded in 1927, the Singanhoe united nationalist and socialist factions into the largest anti-Japanese organization of the colonial era.

Era Japanese Colonial Period Topic Independence Movement Topic Politics Related Japan

Overview

The Singanhoe (新幹會) was an anti-Japanese national organization formed in Seoul in February 1927 through an alliance between nationalist and socialist factions. It operated under three founding principles: "We urge political and economic awakening," "We strengthen our solidarity," and "We utterly reject opportunism." At its founding, Yi Sang-jae served as president and Hong Myeong-hee as vice president. The organization grew into the largest legal body of the Japanese colonial period, with more than 140 branch chapters across the country and a membership approaching 40,000.

Background

After the March First Movement of 1919, the independence movement split into rival nationalist and socialist camps. In the mid-1920s, the Comintern issued a directive (1926) calling for a united front between national liberation movements and nationalist forces in colonized territories, creating conditions for a left-right coalition. At the same time, certain nationalists such as Yi Gwang-su began advocating "autonomism" — a policy of accommodation with Japanese rule — and the non-compromising nationalists who opposed this joined hands with socialist forces.

Major Activities

The Singanhoe promoted national consciousness through nationwide lecture tours and supported labor and peasant movements. When the Gwangju Student Independence Movement broke out in 1929, the organization dispatched a fact-finding mission and attempted to convene a mass rally, but Japanese suppression foiled the effort. Repeated bans on assemblies and arrests of leaders constrained its activities, and in 1931 the organization voted to dissolve, driven by a shift in Comintern policy and internal ideological disputes.

Historical Significance

The Singanhoe demonstrated the possibility of national solidarity across ideological lines, broadening the scope of the independence movement under Japanese rule. Though it survived only four years, the experience of left-right cooperation served as a historical precedent for reunification efforts after liberation, including the Left-Right Coalition Committee of 1946. The women's sister organization Geunuhoe (槿友會) was founded at the same time and became a center of gravity for women's participation in the independence movement.
