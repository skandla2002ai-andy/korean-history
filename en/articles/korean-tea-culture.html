Korean Tea Culture — Monk Uii and the Revival of Dado

The history of Korean tea culture, from its Buddhist origins to the revival of dado (the way of tea) in the late Joseon period.

Era Late Joseon Topic Culture Topic History

Origins and Buddhist Foundations

Tea was introduced to the Korean peninsula from China during the Three Kingdoms period, taking root most deeply within Buddhist monasteries. According to the Samguk Yusa, tea was offered at the altar during the Silla unification wars, and the Goryeo dynasty elevated tea culture to a refined court and monastic art. Elaborate tea rituals accompanied Buddhist ceremonies, and the dasa (茶舍, tea house) was a standard feature of major temples.

Decline During Joseon

The Joseon dynasty's Confucian ideology suppressed Buddhism and, with it, much of the tea culture associated with monastic life. The refined dado (茶道, way of tea) that had flourished in Goryeo contracted dramatically. Tea largely retreated to mountain temples and fell out of mainstream courtly and literary culture.

Monk Uii and the Late Joseon Revival

The late Joseon revival of tea culture is most associated with Monk Uii (意恂, 1786–1866), also known as Cho Ui or Dasan's contemporary. Uii composed the Dongdasong (東茶頌, "Eulogy of Eastern Tea") and Dasinjeon (茶神傳, "Transmission of the Tea Spirit"), establishing a systematic Korean theory of tea aesthetics. He argued that Korean mountain tea, properly prepared, was equal to or superior to Chinese tea. Uii's friendship with the Silhak scholar Dasan Jeong Yak-yong produced a remarkable cross-pollination of tea culture and pragmatic scholarship.

The scholar-official Kim Jeong-hui (Chusa) was another central figure, corresponding with Uii and celebrating tea in his calligraphy and prose. Through these figures, tea became linked to a broader cultural movement valuing authenticity, Korean identity, and the simple life.

Historical Significance

Korean tea culture developed a distinct aesthetic from Chinese and Japanese traditions, emphasizing simplicity, naturalness, and the meditative quality of the ordinary. The late Joseon revival articulated this identity explicitly. Today Korean tea culture — centered on wild green teas from the Jiri Mountain and Boseong regions — continues as a living tradition, and Monk Uii is regarded as its founding theorist.
