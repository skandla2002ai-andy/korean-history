Silla Students in Tang China — Before and After Choe Chiwon

Silla's state-sponsored and private student programs in Tang China. Binggongke exam passers and the East Asian knowledge network.

Era Unified Silla Topic History Topic Culture Related China

Overview

From immediately after the unification of the Three Kingdoms, Unified Silla dispatched students to Tang China. They were divided into state-funded students (sukwi haksaeng, 宿衛學生) and privately funded students (sabiyuhaksaeng, 私費留學生). At the peak, hundreds of Silla students studied at the Tang capital Chang'an's National Academy (Guozijian). They sat the binggongke (賓貢科), a special civil service examination for foreign students, either entering Tang officialdom or returning home to form an intellectual class within Silla's bone-rank society.

The Binggongke and the Student System

The binggongke was a separate civil examination for foreign students; those who passed could receive official posts in Tang. The number of Silla nationals who passed reached several dozen in the 8th–9th centuries—more than any other country in East Asia at the time. The Silla government systematized the dispatch of students, institutionally ensuring that returning scholars became the core intellectuals of the head-rank six (yukdupum) class. These students absorbed Chinese literature, Confucian classics, law, astronomy, and other disciplines and transmitted them to Silla.

Choe Chiwon (崔致遠, 857–?), the most celebrated figure in this tradition, departed for Tang at age twelve and passed the binggongke at the top of his class at eighteen. During the Huang Chao Rebellion he composed the famous "Proclamation Against Huang Chao" (討黃巢檄文), winning great renown. Returning in 885, he submitted a ten-point reform memorial to Queen Jinseong, but when it was ignored he abandoned office and went into seclusion. His collected writings, Gyewon Pilgyeong (桂苑筆耕), are the oldest surviving personal anthology by a Korean author.

Students Before and After Choe Chiwon

Before Choe Chiwon, literary figures such as Gangsu and Seol Chong had advanced Chinese-style writing, and in the 8th century Kim Un-gyeong (金雲卿) became the first Silla student to pass the binggongke, opening the path for others. After Choe Chiwon, Choe Seung-u (崔承祐) and Choe Eon-wi (崔彦撝) became leading intellectuals in the late Silla and early Goryeo periods respectively. These "Three Choes" (Samchoe) exemplify the prototypical Silla head-rank six intellectual. Returning students strengthened Silla's Confucian political ideology and raised the standard of Chinese-language literature, yet the constraints of the bone-rank system denied them positions commensurate with their abilities, making them one axis of the social contradictions that beset Silla in its final decades.

Historical Significance

Silla's Tang study-abroad system was part of the East Asian knowledge network, demonstrating that the Korean peninsula was an active participant in international scholarly exchange. The Confucian political philosophy and literary capabilities brought back by these students formed the foundation of the aristocratic literati culture of Goryeo that followed, and served as a model for the introduction of the civil examination system. The case of Choe Chiwon, moreover, remains emblematic of the late Silla crisis in which the clash between talent and institutional constraints contributed to dynastic change.
