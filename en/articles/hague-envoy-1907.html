The Hague Secret Emissaries — Yi Jun's Last Appeal

In 1907, Emperor Gojong sent three secret envoys to the Hague Peace Conference. Yi Jun died there. Gojong was forced to abdicate.

Era Korean Empire Topic Diplomacy Topic People Related Japan

Overview

The Hague Secret Emissary Incident refers to Emperor Gojong's dispatch of three envoys — Yi Sangsol, Yi Jun, and Yi Wijong — to the Second Hague Peace Conference in the Netherlands in 1907, in a diplomatic bid to expose the illegality of the Eulsa Protectorate Treaty to the international community. Blocked by Japanese diplomatic pressure from attending the conference, the envoys nonetheless met individually with delegates from various nations and held press conferences to publicize Korea's position. Yi Jun died in Hague, while Yi Sangsol and Yi Wijong continued the independence movement abroad. Japan used the incident as a pretext to force Gojong to abdicate and to dissolve the Korean army.

Background of the Mission

Following the Eulsa Protectorate Treaty (1905), the Korean Empire was stripped of its diplomatic rights, closing off official channels of protest. Gojong had been appealing to the great powers through private letters and intermediaries to argue the treaty's illegality. Upon learning that Russian Tsar Nicholas II planned to convene the Hague Peace Conference in 1907, Gojong secretly dispatched Yi Jun, a leader of the Constitutional Research Society; Yi Sangsol, a former state councillor; and Yi Wijong, a staff member of the Korean legation in Russia.

Activities in Hague and Aftermath

The three envoys arrived in Hague in June 1907, but Japanese diplomatic pressure — citing the Eulsa Treaty — resulted in their formal exclusion from the conference. They met individually with delegations from various countries and held press conferences; Yi Wijong delivered a speech in French that attracted considerable international media attention. Yi Jun died in Hague that July. Japan used the incident to force Gojong to abdicate in favor of Crown Prince Sunjong, and the Korea-Japan Agreement of 1907 (the Jeongmi Seven Articles) gave Japan control over Korea's internal administration.

Historical Significance

The Hague mission was an effort to defend sovereignty not by force but through international law and public opinion. Though it produced no direct results, it brought the illegality of the Eulsa Treaty to the attention of the international community and set a precedent for "diplomatic independence activism" — a tradition that continued when Kim Gyusik sought to publicize the March First Movement at the Paris Peace Conference in 1919. The death of patriot Yi Jun remains a powerful symbol of the brutality of Japanese imperial encroachment.
