The Korean War — Three Years of War, Seventy Years of Division

June 25, 1950. North Korean tanks crossed the 38th parallel. Three years of war, then an armistice. Seventy years later, there is still no peace treaty.

Era Modern Topic War & Division Topic International Relations

Overview

The Korean War (韓國戰爭, 1950–1953) began on June 25, 1950, with North Korea's invasion of the South. UN forces (led by the United States) supported South Korea, while the Chinese People's Volunteer Army supported North Korea, expanding the conflict into an international war. An armistice was signed on July 27, 1953, but a peace treaty has never been concluded.

Course of the War

The war shifted rapidly: North Korean surprise invasion (June 25, 1950) → Naktong River defense line (August) → Incheon Landing (September 15, General MacArthur) → advance to the Yalu River (October) → Chinese intervention (October 19) → Seoul recaptured by the North (January 1951) → front lines stalemate (mid-1951 to 1953) → Armistice Agreement (July 27, 1953).

Casualties and Outcome

Combined civilian and military deaths are estimated at approximately 1.5 million on the South Korean side and around 1 million on the North Korean side — enormous losses relative to pre-war populations. Approximately 10 million people were separated from their families. The front lines returned to roughly the same position as the 38th parallel before the war. The conflict is sometimes called "the war that gained nothing," yet it was also the first real test of UN collective security in action.

Key Facts

Outbreak: June 25, 1950 · Incheon Landing: September 15, 1950 · Chinese intervention: October 1950 · Armistice: July 27, 1953 · Deaths: approximately 2.5 million combined (North and South, estimated) · Separated families: approximately 10 million
