The Imjin War and Ming China's Intervention — An East Asian International War

Ming China's deployment of troops turned the Imjin War into an international conflict spanning three nations. The competing interests of Korea, China, and Japan.

Era Joseon Topic War Topic Diplomacy Related China Related Japan

Overview

When the Imjin War broke out in April 1592, Joseon immediately requested military assistance from Ming China. Ming initially dispatched a small force, but after Pyongyang fell, it sent a full army. The conflict thus expanded into an international war involving Korea, Ming China, and Japan — three nations with interlocking strategic interests — and dragged on for seven years.

Background and Course of Ming's Intervention

The Wanli Emperor of Ming concluded that if Japan used Korea as a stepping stone, the Ming heartland itself would be threatened, and ordered troops deployed. In July 1592, a Ming force of 3,000 under Zu Chengxun (祖承訓) entered Korea first, but was badly defeated by Japanese forces at Pyongyang. Ming then sent a large army of 43,000 under Li Rusong (李如松) in January 1593, and in alliance with Korean forces succeeded in retaking Pyongyang.

However, after Li Rusong was defeated by a Japanese vanguard at the Battle of Byeokjegwan (February 1593), he adopted a passive posture. Peace negotiations began between Ming and Japan, and Joseon was effectively excluded from talks held on its own territory. The negotiations dragged on for four years, but Japan's demands — including a Ming princess as bride for the Japanese crown prince and the cession of southern Korea's four provinces — were rejected, leading to the second invasion (Jeongyu War) in 1597.

The Three Nations' Interests and Historical Significance

Ming sought to protect Joseon as a vassal state while preventing Japan from threatening the Chinese mainland. Toyotomi Hideyoshi of Japan harbored ambitions to conquer Ming and seize hegemony over East Asia. Joseon had to seek survival between two great powers; the war killed a significant portion of the population and devastated the country.

Ming's intervention in the Imjin War was decisive in preventing Japan from fully annexing Korea. However, the war severely drained Ming's finances — a factor that later hampered its ability to effectively respond to the rising Jurchen state of Later Jin (後金) in Manchuria. After the war, the East Asian international order began transitioning away from the Ming-centered system toward a new configuration.
