Korean Tofu History — From Goryeo to the Table

How tofu arrived in Korea from China during the Goryeo period, took root in Buddhist temple cuisine, and became a staple of Korean daily life.

Era Goryeo Topic Culture Topic History Related China

Introduction from China

Tofu (두부, dubu) was introduced to Korea from China, most likely during the Goryeo period (918–1392). The earliest Korean record mentioning tofu appears in the writings of Yi Saek (李穡, 1328–1396), the late Goryeo scholar, who praised it as a humble but nourishing food. Tofu was likely transmitted through Buddhist monks travelling between China and Korea, as it fit perfectly into the vegetarian diet of temple cuisine.

Tofu in Goryeo Buddhist Culture

Goryeo was a deeply Buddhist state, and Buddhist dietary prohibitions against meat made bean-based protein sources essential. Tofu — made from soybeans abundant in Korea — became a key part of temple food (sacheol eumsik). Monasteries likely played a central role in spreading tofu-making techniques across the country. The Goryeo aristocracy also adopted tofu as part of refined cuisine, sometimes listing it alongside rare ingredients in literary works.

Joseon: From Temple to Table

During the Joseon period, tofu production spread widely among commoners. Dubu became affordable everyday food, prepared in countless ways — braised (dubu jorim), in soup (sundubu jjigae), pan-fried (dubu buchim), or eaten fresh with soy sauce. The royal court cuisine also featured elaborately prepared tofu dishes. By the mid-Joseon period, tofu was firmly embedded in Korean food culture at all levels of society.

Tofu production was organized commercially in towns; street tofu vendors became a familiar part of urban life. Regional variations in texture and flavor developed — soft silken tofu, firmer pressed varieties, and the distinctly Korean sundubu (순두부, soft uncurdled tofu) each found dedicated culinary applications.

Historical Significance

The story of tofu in Korea illustrates how a foreign food technology, transmitted through religious and scholarly networks, can be absorbed so completely that it becomes an inseparable part of national cuisine. Today Korean tofu dishes — from the spicy sundubu jjigae to the banchan staple of braised tofu — are recognized globally. The humble bean curd that arrived with Buddhist monks in the Goryeo era remains one of Korea's most beloved and versatile ingredients.
