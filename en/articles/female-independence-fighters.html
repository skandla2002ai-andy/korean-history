Female Independence Fighters of the Japanese Colonial Period

Beyond Yu Gwan-sun, countless women fought for Korean independence. Nam Ja-hyeon, Jeong Jeong-hwa, Kim Maria, and many others.

Era Japanese Colonial Topic Person Topic Independence Movement Related Japan

The March First Movement and Women's Awakening

The history of women's participation in the independence movement during the Japanese colonial period began in earnest with the March First Movement of 1919. Thousands of women joined the independence demonstrations, with students from girls' schools such as Ewha Haktang and Baehwa Haktang playing central roles. Among them, Yu Gwan-sun (柳寬順, 1902–1920), a student at Ewha Haktang, led demonstrations at the Aunae Marketplace in her hometown of Cheonan, was arrested, and died in Seodaemun Prison. She remained defiant despite brutal torture and has become a symbol of indomitable resistance; after the Republic of Korea was established, she was posthumously honored as a patriot. The March First Movement was the first occasion on which women publicly and collectively joined the national movement.

Women Fighters at Home and Abroad

Nam Ja-hyeon (南慈賢, 1872–1933) was a female independence activist in Manchuria and one of the rare cases of direct armed resistance. When a League of Nations investigation team visited Manchuria, she severed her own finger and attempted to present a blood-written petition for independence; she was later arrested in Harbin while attempting to assassinate a senior Japanese official and died in custody. An Gyeong-sin threw a bomb at the Japanese Governor-General's building in Pyongyang in 1920, an act of armed resistance by a female independence fighter. In the Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea, Jeong Jeong-hwa (鄭靖和) traveled between Shanghai and Korea transporting independence movement funds, earning the name "Mother of the Independence Army."

The Geunuhoe and Women's Organizations

The Geunuhoe (槿友會), founded in 1927, was the largest women's organization formed by an alliance of nationalist and socialist women's groups. Its main goals included women's enlightenment, the abolition of feudal customs, and the protection of women workers' rights; it operated branches across the country and ran lectures and night schools. It functioned as the sister organization to the Singanhoe (a male left-right alliance), and effectively dissolved in 1931 when the Singanhoe was disbanded. The Geunuhoe holds an important place in the history of Korean women's movements for pursuing both independence and women's liberation simultaneously.

Historical Significance and Reassessment

Female independence fighters of the Japanese colonial period were long marginalized in historical accounts. Many have only recently been identified and posthumously honored. According to records from the Ministry of Patriots and Veterans Affairs, the proportion of women among officially recognized independence patriots has been increasing but remains far lower than men — expanding research into women's roles in the independence movement remains an ongoing task.
