Forced Labor — Korean Workers in Japanese Mines and Factories

Korean workers forcibly mobilized to Japan during the Pacific War. The unresolved historical issue and its present-day dimensions.

Era Japanese Colonial Topic Society Topic History Related Japan

Overview

The forced mobilization of Korean labor refers to Japan's systematic conscription of Koreans under the National General Mobilization Law (1938) from 1939 until liberation in 1945. Koreans were sent to mines, munitions factories, and construction sites in the Japanese mainland, Sakhalin, and the South Pacific. The officially tallied number of mobilized workers is approximately 660,000, though estimates including unofficial mobilization exceed one million. The practice was accompanied by severe human rights abuses — brutal working conditions, little or no pay, and physical violence — and many workers died before they could return home.

Methods and Conditions of Mobilization

In its early phase the mobilization was a quasi-compulsory "recruitment" system, but it shifted to government-mediated placement in 1942 and legal forced conscription under the National Requisition Ordinance from 1944. In coal mines, workers labored more than ten hours a day under extreme conditions; injuries and deaths on the job were common. Wages were largely withheld under the guise of forced savings and deductions, and the vast majority of victims never received their unpaid wages or savings after liberation.

Post-War Settlement and Current Issues

Under the 1965 Treaty on Basic Relations and Claims Agreement, Japan provided South Korea with $500 million in economic cooperation funds. The Japanese government maintains that this settlement "completely and finally" resolved all claims. The Korean Supreme Court ruled differently, however — in 2012 and again in a final ruling in 2018 — holding that individuals' claims for compensation for illegal acts were not extinguished by the Claims Agreement. These rulings became the central flashpoint in Korean–Japanese diplomatic tensions, which continue to the present day.

Historical Significance

The forced labor issue is an ongoing, unresolved matter that illuminates the tension between individual redress for colonial-era harm and interstate treaty frameworks — between historical justice and diplomatic settlement. Discussions continue over asset seizures against companies such as Nippon Steel (formerly Shin Nippon Seitetsu) and Mitsubishi, as well as diplomatic solutions including third-party settlement mechanisms. Restoring the honor of surviving victims and establishing the full historical record remain crucial tasks in Korean–Japanese relations.
